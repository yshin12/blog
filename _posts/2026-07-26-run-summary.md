### Analysis

This was a textbook three-stage progression over 22 km. The first 8 km sat in a genuinely
restrained 6:34–6:51 /km window with HR barely above 100 bpm — an unusually patient opening.
The middle block (km 9–15) settled into 5:20–5:50 /km with HR in the low 120s, and the final
third (km 16–21) rolled down to 4:48–5:04 /km before a deliberate two-kilometre cool-down.

The standout number is cardiac efficiency. Even at 4:48 /km — the quickest kilometre of the day —
HR only reached 141–143 bpm, and the session max was 146 bpm across more than two hours of
running. Stride length tells the same story, opening from 0.85 m to 1.17 m while cadence stayed
pinned in a tight 168–178 spm band. That is speed generated by stride extension rather than by
turnover panic, which is the efficient way to do it.

### Effort & Medical Note

Current cardiology guidance is **easy effort only — no tempo, threshold, intervals, or
progression finishes** until harder work is formally cleared. On the HR side this run behaved
impeccably: 121 avg, 146 max, no drift, no spike. But structurally, the closing six kilometres
at 4:48–5:04 /km *are* a progression finish, which is the one thing the current restriction
names explicitly. The distance itself is fine — the 5 km cap has long expired and 22 km is
within reach now. The suggestion is simply to keep long runs flat in pace: if the legs feel good
late, hold the easy pace and enjoy the freshness rather than converting it into a fast finish.
Worth raising the progression question at the upcoming cardiology follow-up so the ceiling is
defined rather than guessed at.

At 2:07 and 1,571 kcal, this is also the largest single fuelling demand in weeks. Given that
hypoglycemia was a contributing factor on 2026-06-06, carbohydrate intake during any run past
90 minutes should be non-negotiable, along with steady fluids in July heat.
