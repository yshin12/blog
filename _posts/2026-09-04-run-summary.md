One activity today in Burlington: a long, steadily accelerating 20.32 km (12.63 mi) run on a
warm but dry late afternoon — 25 °C (77 °F) with a low 13.9 °C (57 °F) dew point and almost no
wind.

**Run type: Endurance — progression long run (LR).** The Pfitzinger endurance band is
4:52–5:24 /km (7:50–8:41 /mi), explicitly meant to be run "progressing faster through the run."
With 25 °C but a dry 57 °F dew point, the temp + dew-point sum is only 134 °F — below the 140
threshold, so the heat penalty is modest, perhaps 2–3%, giving a heat-equivalent band of about
5:00–5:33 /km (8:03–8:56 /mi). Against that, this run was executed at the fast end and then
past it.

### Progression structure

| Segment | Distance | Avg pace | Avg HR |
|---|---|---|---|
| First 10 km | 10.00 km (6.21 mi) | 5:07 /km (8:14 /mi) | ~130 bpm |
| Second 10 km | 10.00 km (6.21 mi) | 4:47 /km (7:42 /mi) | ~145 bpm |
| Final 5 km | 5.00 km (3.11 mi) | 4:40 /km (7:31 /mi) | ~149 bpm |
| Closing 320 m | 0.32 km (0.20 mi) | 4:21 /km (7:00 /mi) | 157 bpm |

This is a textbook negative split: 20 seconds per kilometre faster over the back half, with
not one split slower than the one before it after the opening kilometre. The pace ladder runs
5:22 → 5:13 → 5:10 → 5:07 → 5:09 → 5:04 → 4:59 → ... → 4:41 → 4:42 → 4:40 → 4:38 → 4:36, and
then a 4:21 /km (7:00 /mi) kick to finish. Only two splits in twenty-one were slower than their
predecessor, and both by under five seconds — remarkable control over 100 minutes.

Heart rate tells the same story from the other side. It opened at 114 bpm and finished at
157 bpm, but it rose *with* the pace rather than drifting ahead of it: 130 bpm through the easy
first third, 140 bpm in the middle, 150s only in the last four kilometres. Average 137 bpm for
a 100-minute run is a genuinely aerobic day, and max 160 bpm never approached threshold
territory. The effort stayed under control the whole way.

The mechanics were the best part. Cadence climbed from 172 spm in the opening kilometre to
189 spm at the finish, while stride length simultaneously *lengthened* from 1.08 m to 1.20 m.
Speeding up through both levers at once — more turnover and longer stride — is what a run
finishing fresh looks like; the fatigue signature would be the opposite. Moving time was
1:40:14 against 1:40:20 elapsed: six seconds of stopping in an hour and forty minutes.

## Plan note

Friday of Pfitzinger week 5 (Aug 31 – Sep 6, 97 km target) prescribes **recovery — 8 km
(5 mi)**. What was actually run is **Thursday's endurance 19 km (11.8 mi)**, displaced one day
by the earlier LT swap. Judged against the swapped intent, adherence is strong — 20.32 km
against 19 km prescribed, run as a progression when a flat endurance run was all that was
asked. The cost is that the week now has no true recovery day between Wednesday's tempo and
Sunday's 26 km long run.

That matters for what comes next. Saturday is general aerobic 14 km (8.7 mi) with 10 × 100 m
strides, and Sunday is the progression long run — 26 km (16.1 mi) finishing with 5 km at LT
pace, 4:03–4:09 /km (6:31–6:41 /mi). Coming off a tempo Thursday and a fast-finish 20 km today,
Saturday should be run at the genuinely slow end of the general aerobic band, 5:30–5:40 /km
(8:51–9:07 /mi), with the strides kept short and relaxed rather than sharp. Sunday's LT finish
is the week's real target and it needs the legs.

On the left hamstring/glute: today loaded the tendon meaningfully — the last five kilometres
at 1.16–1.20 m stride length carry real hip extension force — but the data shows nothing
concerning. No split anomalies, no cadence collapse, no protective shortening. Isometric holds
tonight rather than stretching. If the sit-bone area is sore on waking, take Saturday very easy
and drop the strides; if it lingers into Sunday, the 26 km can stand without its LT finish.

Five weeks to October 11.
